# Wilde

**Furthest from neutral:** epigram · inversion of received wisdom · surface polish that treats seriousness as vulgar

- **Rhythm** — balanced and antithetical. Sentences fall into two halves that mirror or oppose. The rhythm of a delivered line, timed for a listener.
- **Syntax** — symmetrical. Parallel construction, chiasmus, reversal. The characteristic sentence takes a platitude and swaps its terms so the result sounds equally authoritative and means the opposite.
- **Distance** — in dialogue, none; characters are vehicles for wit. In prose, an amused narrator slightly above the proceedings, giving beauty and folly equal attention.
- **Selection** — surfaces. Clothes, flowers, rooms, objects, the appearance of people, rendered lavishly. Interiority and motive withheld. Consequence arrives late and abruptly.
- **Emotion** — deflected by wit until it isn't. The move: a paragraph of sustained levity broken by one plain sentence that means it. The contrast does the work.
- **Dialogue** — the main event. Characters trade epigrams, cap each other, compete. Nobody speaks plainly if a paradox is available. Deliberately more mannered than realistic.
- **Paragraph** — moderate, poised, often closing on the epigram.
- **Diction** — elegant, Latinate, precise. Vocabulary of beauty, taste, sin, society. No coarseness; the register stays polished even when the content isn't.
- **Figures** — aesthetic similes from art, flowers, jewels, music. Decorative and admitted to be so.
- **Unusual** — the inverted maxim that sounds wiser than the truth it displaced. The tonal switch: sustained frivolity making one sincere line land with disproportionate force.

**Writing it:** build sentences in balanced halves. Invert a received wisdom rather than stating one. Withhold sincerity, then spend it once. Don't scatter aphorisms indiscriminately — his paradoxes are structurally sound, not merely contrary.
