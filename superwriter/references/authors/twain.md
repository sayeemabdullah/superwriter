# Twain

**Furthest from neutral:** vernacular first person · deadpan delivery · satire through unreliable innocence

- **Rhythm** — spoken. Sentences run on as talk runs on, joined by *and* and *so*, paced like someone telling a story aloud to a present listener. Digression is structural.
- **Syntax** — paratactic and loose. Grammar bends toward speech: fragments, comma splices where a speaker would push on, dialect spellings held consistently. Complexity from accumulation, not subordination.
- **Distance** — first person, close, with a narrator whose understanding is narrower than the reader's. The gap between what he reports and what the reader infers does all the satirical work.
- **Selection** — river, road, weather, practical detail, the mechanics of getting by. Social observation delivered as plain reporting. Little abstraction — he notices, he doesn't theorize.
- **Emotion** — understated, deflected by humour. Serious moments arrive in the same register as the jokes and are stronger for it. Sentiment undercut before it settles.
- **Dialogue** — extensive, dialect-marked, individuated by region and class. Characters argue about nonsense at length. Timing depends on the straight-faced reply.
- **Paragraph** — loose and conversational, following the turns of the telling.
- **Diction** — colloquial, regional, concrete. Deliberate misspelling and grammatical error rendered consistently as speech. Formal diction only in parody.
- **Figures** — homely comparisons from farm, river, small-town life. Exaggeration used structurally: the tall tale escalating past belief with a straight face.
- **Unusual** — the moral inversion: a narrator who believes he's doing wrong while doing right, reporting his guilt sincerely. The deadpan — the funniest line delivered with no acknowledgment it was one.

**Writing it:** write it as spoken. Let the narrator understand less than the reader. Deliver the joke without flagging it. Keep dialect consistent or drop it — inconsistent dialect reads as mockery.
