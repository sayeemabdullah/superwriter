# Austen

**Furthest from neutral:** free indirect style · ironic distance · judgment placed inside apparently neutral report

- **Rhythm** — balanced, periodic. Moderate length, symmetrical clauses, often in pairs or triples. Conversational but formal.
- **Syntax** — hypotactic and controlled. Qualifications nest neatly. Sentences turn on a semicolon or conjunction that reverses the first half's implication. The shape itself carries the irony.
- **Distance** — free indirect style is the defining feature. Narration slips into a character's idiom unannounced, so the reader can't tell whether a judgment is the character's or the author's. Shifts within a paragraph, sometimes within a sentence.
- **Selection** — interiors, money, manners. Almost no landscape, weather, or physical description beyond what social evaluation needs. What people say and mean; rarely what they look like.
- **Emotion** — held in irony and in what characters decline to say. Direct statement of feeling is rare and lands hard because of it.
- **Dialogue** — abundant, individuated. Foolish characters are foolish in specific non-interchangeable ways. Speech reveals class, education, self-deception. Plain tags. Long speeches given to characters who talk too much; the length is the joke.
- **Paragraph** — moderate, unified, building to a turn in the last sentence.
- **Diction** — formal, Latinate, precise. Abstract nouns for qualities and conditions. Very few adverbs.
- **Figures** — sparse. Largely literal; effects come from structure and irony.
- **Unusual** — judgment delivered as though it were fact: a character's opinion of herself reported so flatly the report becomes the verdict. The opening maxim stated with total confidence that the work then dismantles.

**Writing it:** free indirect style before anything else. Let sentences turn against themselves at the semicolon. Judge without stating the judgment. Don't add landscape or physical description — she wouldn't.
