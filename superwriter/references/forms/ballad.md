# Ballad

**Furthest from neutral:** the ballad stanza's swing · story told in leaps, not transitions · impersonal voice and incremental repetition

- **Meter** — alternating four-stress and three-stress iambic lines (common measure). The rhythm is song-derived and insistent; small irregularities are absorbed by the tune.
- **Lineation** — the line follows the beat. The short trimeter line lands hard because a beat is missing where the ear expects one.
- **Rhyme** — the second and fourth lines rhyme (ABCB); ABAB is tighter and rarer. Rhyme is plain and often slant; momentum matters more than a perfect chime.
- **Stanza** — the quatrain. Each stanza is roughly one scene or one exchange, and the story jumps between stanzas with nothing in between.
- **Enjambment vs end-stop** — heavily end-stopped, line and stanza both. Enjambment is rare and would break the song.
- **Turn** — carried by the refrain: a repeated line or stanza that returns changed — incremental repetition — each return advancing or darkening the story.
- **Sound** — plain and singable; alliteration light. The repetition does the ear's work.
- **Compression** — severe, by omission. Motive, transition, and aftermath are cut; the story is dialogue and event, and the listener fills the gaps.
- **Selection** — one dramatic action: a death, a betrayal, a return. No backstory, no stated moral, no narrator personality — the teller does not judge, and the dread is greater for it.
- **Diction** — plain, concrete, a little archaic by tradition, but syntax stays simple.

**Writing it:** tell it in jumps, scene to scene with the connective tissue cut. Keep the four-three beat and let the trimeter line thud. Use a refrain that changes on each return. Explaining the characters' feelings, or smoothing the transitions, turns a ballad into a verse short story.
