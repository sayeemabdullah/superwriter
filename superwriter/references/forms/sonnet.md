# Sonnet

**Furthest from neutral:** the volta · a rhyme scheme tight enough to be felt as structure · a whole argument resolved in fourteen lines

- **Meter** — iambic pentameter, kept closely enough that departures register. A trochaic first foot for a stressed opening; a feminine ending for hesitation. Metronomic regularity is a fault, not a virtue.
- **Lineation** — the line is mostly a unit of sense; a strong enjambment is a deliberate event, usually near the turn. The pentameter line wants a caesura somewhere near its middle.
- **Rhyme** — structural, not ornamental. Italian: ABBA ABBA / CDE CDE, the octave sealed, the sestet freer. English: ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. Slant rhyme is allowed; no rhyme is a different form.
- **Stanza** — no printed breaks, but the form is jointed: octave against sestet (Italian), or 4-4-4-2 (English). The argument must feel those joints.
- **Enjambment vs end-stop** — moderate. Quatrains tend to end-stop; the closing couplet almost always does.
- **Turn** — the defining axis. Italian: between line 8 and line 9 — problem then resolution, question then answer. English: often delayed to the couplet, which snaps the twelve preceding lines shut or undercuts them.
- **Sound** — present but not foregrounded; the rhyme carries the ear. Heavy alliteration reads as a different tradition.
- **Compression** — real but not violent; fourteen lines is room to breathe. Inversion to reach a rhyme is the first thing a reader notices.
- **Selection** — one situation, one argument. A sonnet holding two is two poems.
- **Diction** — contemporary. *Doth* and *ere* over a modern sentence is the pure caricature.

**Writing it:** decide where the turn falls and build the whole poem around that hinge. Let the argument, not the rhyme, choose the words, then adjust for rhyme last — slant rhyme before inversion. Filler syllables padding the meter and a final couplet grabbing for an epigram it has not earned are the two failures a reader spots at once.
