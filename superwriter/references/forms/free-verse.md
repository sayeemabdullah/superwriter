# Free Verse

**Furthest from neutral:** lineation as the only fixed technique · the line-break carrying rhythm, emphasis, and syntax at once · cadence organised by breath and image, not by count

- **Meter** — none imposed. Rhythm comes from cadence — the natural stress of the phrase, the length of a breath — and often from a loose recurring beat the poem sets itself and is free to break.
- **Lineation** — the whole craft. Every break is a choice: on a noun to weight it, before a verb to suspend it, against the syntax to make the reader hesitate. It is the only guaranteed tool, so it must never be arbitrary.
- **Rhyme** — incidental. Internal rhyme, slant rhyme, and repetition may bind the poem; a regular end-rhyme scheme would make it another form.
- **Stanza** — variable, and meaningful because it is chosen: a rest, a jump-cut, a change of address. Uniform stanzas here are a decision, not a default.
- **Enjambment vs end-stop** — set poem by poem. Enjambment is the norm; a hard end-stop in a mostly enjambed poem lands like a full stop in speech.
- **Turn** — no formal turn, but strong free verse still turns — on an image, a shift of address, one short line after long ones.
- **Sound** — used freely and locally: a run of assonance for a few lines, then dropped. Not sustained as structure.
- **Compression** — variable, from Imagist spareness to long-lined accumulation; whichever the poem picks, it holds within itself.
- **Selection** — carries the weight meter and rhyme would share in formal verse. The images must be exact; nothing else holds the poem up.
- **Figurative habit** — often the load-bearing element: one precise image can be the whole poem.

**Writing it:** make every line-break do a job — rhythm, emphasis, or a pull against the grammar — and be able to name that job for each one. Let cadence and image organise the poem where meter would. Prose chopped into lines at intervals that would fall the same anywhere is the caricature, and the most common one.
