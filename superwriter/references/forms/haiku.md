# Haiku

**Furthest from neutral:** the cut between two images · concrete present-tense perception with no comment · radical compression

- **Meter** — none in English. The received "5-7-5 syllables" mistranslates the Japanese sound-unit; serious English haiku run shorter, roughly a short–long–short of 2–3 / 3–4 / 2–3 stresses. Counting to seventeen is not the form.
- **Lineation** — three short lines, short–long–short. The breaks mark the two parts the poem sets side by side.
- **Rhyme** — none. Rhyme forces the diction and destroys the plainness.
- **Stanza** — the whole poem is one three-line stanza; the white space around it is part of it.
- **Enjambment vs end-stop** — the two images are each end-stopped in effect; within an image a phrase may run across a line.
- **Turn** — the *kireji* or cutting word: a break, usually after line 1 or line 2, that sets two images beside each other without stating their relation. The unstated relation is the poem. No cut, no haiku.
- **Sound** — quiet: occasional assonance, nothing that calls attention.
- **Compression** — extreme. Articles, most verbs, and connectives drop; what remains is two things-in-a-setting and the charge between them.
- **Selection** — one observed moment, usually with a season or time-of-day marker grounding it. No abstraction, no simile, no *like*.
- **Diction** — plain, concrete, present tense; the first person nearly absent, feeling implied by the image and never named.

**Writing it:** find two concrete images and set them side by side with a cut between them; let the reader supply the connection. Ground it in a season or a time of day. Keep it short — well under seventeen syllables. Counting to 5-7-5, adding a simile, or naming the feeling are the three ways it goes wrong.
