# Form Dimensions

Companion to `craft-dimensions.md`, for verse. A form request reads this file plus one
`references/forms/<name>.md` — not `craft-dimensions.md`. The three prose axes that still
matter for verse are carried at the foot of this file.

A form is a set of positions on these axes. Meeting the syllable count is not writing the
form; the pressure the count puts on syntax and selection is.

| # | Dimension | What to attend to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Meter** | Base foot and line length; how strictly kept; where substitution is licensed (a trochee in the first foot, a feminine ending). Metrical *tension* — a natural stress fighting the meter — is where meaning shows. |
| 2 | **Lineation** | Where lines break and why. The line as a unit of sense (breaking at a clause) vs. of sound (breaking mid-phrase for pull). What the eye does at the right margin. |
| 3 | **Rhyme** | Scheme and its exactness — full, slant, none. Structural (marks the form) vs. ornamental. Rhyme that arrives early (couplet) closes; rhyme delayed (envelope) holds open. |
| 4 | **Stanza** | Size, and whether the argument maps to it — one thought per stanza, or thought spilling across the break. White space as a rest. |
| 5 | **Enjambment vs end-stop** | The ratio, and what a run-on line does: it speeds the read and throws weight onto the first word of the next line. End-stopping slows and seals. |
| 6 | **Turn** | The pivot the form expects — the sonnet's volta, the ballad's refrain, the couplet's sting — and *where* it falls. A form is often defined by the position of its turn. |
| 7 | **Sound** | Alliteration, assonance, consonance; density relative to the form's norm (high in Anglo-Saxon-descended verse, low in plain lyric). Sound binding unrhymed lines. |
| 8 | **Compression** | What is cut to fit: articles, connectives, the second half of a comparison. Inversion and ellipsis under metrical pressure — deliberate, not padding. |

## Carried from craft-dimensions.md

- **Selection** — what the poem includes and refuses. Verse selects harder than prose; an image earns its line or goes.
- **Figurative habit** — frequency and kind; whether images come from one domain; whether the poem is largely literal.
- **Diction register** — Latinate vs Anglo-Saxon, abstraction, archaism. Adjust last: archaic diction over a modern rhythm is the caricature in every form.

## Using these

Name the two or three axes the form is strict about and write to those; let the rest sit at
the form's ordinary. A sonnet is strict on meter, rhyme, and turn — not on sound. Free verse
is strict on lineation and little else. Maximizing every axis produces the exam answer, not
the poem.
